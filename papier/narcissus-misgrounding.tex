\title{Narcissus Misgrounding: When Models Answer Without the Fact a Reform Made Decisive}

\markboth{}{}

\author[A]{\fnms{Daniel} \snm{Guez}\thanks{Corresponding author: Daniel Guez, Talia, Paris, France.}}
and
\author[A]{\fnms{Rose} \snm{Cymbler}}

\runningauthor{D. Guez and R. Cymbler}
\address[A]{Talia, Paris, France}

\begin{abstract}
We identify and measure narcissus misgrounding: language models answer a legal question without the fact that decides it, having supplied that fact itself. Nothing is fabricated, the law cited is correct, and the applicable version has been retrieved; what is missing is a fact about the case, which no corpus contains. The models know how to qualify an answer, but not on what is missing.

We build a benchmark of thirteen items across six reforms of French tax law. Each item is a file put to the model as a client would put it to counsel, paired with the version of the text applicable at the date of the facts, so that retrieval is not what is under test. Six items carry a fact the reform has made necessary and the file does not state; seven control items carry none. Thirteen models, three repetitions, 500 responses across 507 cells.

Across five of the six measurement items, those built from reported cases, models request the missing fact in only 3\% of responses (5 of 192); on three of them the fact is never requested, and nine of the thirteen models never ask for it at all. The single outlier driving this figure to 16\% is an AI-generated item, which may indicate that models recognize patterns from their training distribution rather than performing genuine legal reasoning. They do not ask indiscriminately either: on control items, where nothing is missing, they answer rather than ask in 96.5\% of cases, apart from one item where they ask the question a later version of the text would call for.

Read by area, the request rate is the same on both sides of each reform: what models ask follows the subject of the file rather than the new requirements of the law. Coding was verified by a second author on 135 cells drawn blind, including all five positive cases that produce the headline figure. The benchmark, all responses, the coding and the code are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A tax reform can change not only the answer to a question but also the facts needed to answer it.

Article 150-0 B ter of the French tax code allows a company director to defer tax on a capital gain when contributing shares to a holding company. If the holding company subsequently sells those shares within three years, it must reinvest most of the proceeds in an active economic business. Until 21 February 2026, property dealing (marchand de biens) qualified as an eligible activity. From that date, it is excluded.

Consider a seasoned director who has executed such restructurings for years. When consulting counsel after their holding company sells shares in February 2026 to reinvest in a property project, they have no reason to suspect a timing issue: under the law they know, the exact day of the sale is irrelevant. Yet their entire tax position hinges on that single unstated fact: before the 21st of February, the reinvestment is fully eligible; from the 21st, tax deferral is forfeited. A human lawyer immediately asks for the exact date. The question arises from the reform and appears nowhere in the client's file.

We report what thirteen state-of-the-art language models do when placed in counsel's position.

\subsection*{The phenomenon}

We call it narcissus misgrounding: a model answers a legal question without requesting the fact that decides it, having silently supplied that fact itself. In the vocabulary of argumentation theory, the model covertly discharges a burden of establishment, supplying the very premise it should have questioned.

Narcissus is neither blind nor misinformed, and nothing in the water is hidden from him. Held spellbound by his own reflection, he never looks outward. A language model given the applicable statutory text is in that state: it looks at what it already holds rather than at the client's case. What it lacks is a factual premise it could obtain by asking. Instead it fills the void from the default case, the ordinary practice, the scenario its training data suggests. The gap closes without a trace.

This makes the failure more dangerous than hallucination, which a reader can catch. A narcissus-misgrounded answer appears pristine: correct law, right version, seamless reasoning, resting on an assumed fact that silently invalidates the advice.

\subsection*{Contributions}

\emph{Conceptualisation}: we identify and characterize narcissus misgrounding as a structural failure mode distinct from retrieval failure and hallucination. \emph{Benchmark}: we build thirteen legal files across six French tax reforms, each paired with the statutory text in force at the date of the facts, and evaluate thirteen leading models over three repetitions; all 573 responses (500 from the main campaign and 73 from the prompt ablation reported in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), annotations, and code are released.\footnote{Available at \url{https://github.com/DanielGuez16/narcissus-misgrounding}.} \emph{Findings}: across the five reported-case items, models request the decisive fact in only 3\% of responses, and nine of thirteen never ask; the overall 16\% rate is driven by a single AI-generated outlier, which may indicate that models recognize patterns from their training distribution rather than engaging in true legal elicitation. \emph{Resilience to prompting}: the failure survives an explicit instruction to ask, which suggests that the error stems from an inability to perceive factual voids created by legal change. \emph{Observations on the instruments}: we report three deployment anomalies, unenforced token ceilings, non-termination, and undocumented routing variability.

\subsection*{Why this is not a retrieval problem}

Temporal Misgrounding in Legal RAG~\cite{cymbler2026} demonstrated that a versioned corpus with date-conditioned retrieval recovers the applicable statutory version where standard retrieval defaults to the current text. Every prompt in this study directly provides the applicable legal text. The model holds the exact law in front of it. What it lacks is a client-specific fact, one that resides in no legal database or corpus, but must be requested.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Asking for what is missing.}

Steenhuis \& Westermann~\cite{steenhuis2024} test whether an LLM requests missing information during legal aid intake; their most frequent error is asking unnecessary questions where a file could be decided. Two elements differ: their prompt explicitly commands asking, and their missing facts are statically enumerated by eligibility rules. Their work concerns guided intake, whereas we evaluate open advice. Steenhuis~\cite{steenhuis2025} extends this to triage, and Steenhuis and Harvey~\cite{steenhuis2026} extend it further, evaluating the quality of the follow-up questions themselves. Yu et al.~\cite{yu2025} study interactive legal consultations, and Zhang et al.~\cite{dlawbench} evaluate legal consultation over several turns, where the exchange with the client is the object rather than a single answer. Outside law, QuestBench~\cite{questbench} and MediQ~\cite{mediq} test question-asking when required variables are fixed a priori. AskBench~\cite{askbench} measures the same behaviour outside law: models skip the clarification even where asking would improve the answer. The abstention literature measures whether models decline unanswerable questions~\cite{rajpurkar2018}, while Guitton et al.~\cite{guitton2025} measure textual ambiguity in rules. In our benchmark, the required variable is fixed only after a legislative reform alters the legal threshold.

\paragraph{Argumentation theory.}

Argumentation schemes carry critical questions~\cite{waltonschemes2008} required before accepting conclusions. Carneades formalizes these as premises carrying specific burdens of proof~\cite{gordon2007}. Applying a scheme without raising its critical question leaves it dependent on an unstated assumption. Furthermore, Walton \& Krabbe~\cite{waltonkrabbe1995} distinguish information-seeking dialogue from persuasion dialogue; the evaluated models bypass the former entirely.

\paragraph{Norm change and the temporal dimension of law.}

A legal change can alter the conditions of a rule as well as its outcome. Governatori \& Rotolo~\cite{governatori2010} separate abrogation from annulment in defeasible logic. Tamargo et al.~\cite{tamargo2019} formalize temporalized belief revision, extending Lindahl \& Odelstad~\cite{lindahl2003} and Moguillansky et al.~\cite{moguillansky2024}. Palmirani et al.~\cite{palmirani2010,palmirani2009} and Sz\H{o}ke et al.~\cite{szoke2012} model legal temporal dimensions and change management. Slingerland et al.~\cite{slingerland2018} measure reform impact on case classification, and de Martim~\cite{demartim2025} proposes point-in-time reconstruction for legal AI. We measure how models behave when one of these newly required conditions goes missing.

\paragraph{Reliability of language models in law.}

Studies document persistent legal AI fragilities: uncorrected user premises~\cite{dahl2024}, unsupported RAG citations~\cite{magesh2025}, and response instability at zero temperature~\cite{blairstanek2025}. Models fail on explicit statutory rules~\cite{blairstanek2023}, subsumption~\cite{wardas2025}, and detecting overruled precedent~\cite{zhang2025}, while Gultekin et al.~\cite{gultekin2025} decouple factual accuracy from reasoning quality. LegalBench~\cite{legalbench} unifies such tasks. Crucially, these benchmarks evaluate answer correctness or abstention; we measure an upstream failure: whether the model registers that it cannot answer without eliciting missing facts. Lou and Shin~\cite{lou2026} make a related point: legal benchmarks feed models clean expert inputs, and hide the gap with what a layperson actually brings.

\paragraph{What has not been measured.}

Across the venues and period surveyed, we find no study evaluating whether a legal AI system actively requests factual information that a legislative reform has newly made decisive.\footnote{We searched the full proceedings of JURIX 2023 to 2025, ICAIL 2023, 2025 and 2026 (including its workshops), and the journal Artificial Intelligence and Law since 2023.}

\section{The case}

A company director sells their business in two steps: they contribute their shares to a holding company, and the holding company subsequently sells them.

The shares cost the director one million euros, contributed at an agreed valuation of nine; less than three years later, the holding company sells them for nine million euros.

\paragraph{The contribution, and why tax is deferred.}

For tax purposes, a contribution of shares is treated as a sale. The eight-million-euro capital gain is in principle taxable even though the director received no cash, creating an immediate tax liability of 2.4 million euros without liquid funds to pay it. Article 150-0 B ter of the French Tax Code defers this tax liability.

\paragraph{The disposal, and the obligation it creates.}

The holding company no longer owns a business; it holds nine million euros in cash. The director has personally sold nothing, retaining their shares in the holding company. Because the disposal occurred within three years of the initial contribution, the statute requires the holding company to reinvest a substantial portion of the sale proceeds into an active economic business. How much must be reinvested, and within what timeframe, are conditions directly altered by the reform: 60\% within two years for disposals prior to 21 February 2026, versus 70\% within three years for disposals from that date onward. That represents a choice between placing 5.4 million euros within two years or 6.3 million euros within three. The reform also raises the minimum holding period on what is acquired, from twelve months to five years.

\paragraph{Where the lawyer stops and the system proceeds.}

The director consults their tax counsel:

\begin{quote}
\emph{I contributed my shares to my holding company, and it sold them in February 2026. I want to reinvest in a company that buys buildings, renovates them, and resells them. Does that qualify?}
\end{quote}

The client states everything they believe to be relevant. One decisive fact is missing, omitted precisely because they have no reason to know it matters: on which exact day in February did the holding company complete the sale?

Article 11 of the amending act provides, in an uncodified transitional clause, that the reform applies to disposals executed from the day after publication. The law was published on 20 February; the legal boundary falls strictly on the 21st. Prior to the 21st, the eligible reinvestment scope includes any commercial activity under Articles 34 and 35 of the code, under which property dealing (marchand de biens) qualifies. From the 21st onward, the text references a provision excluding real-estate activities. After that date, the project fails.

A system with a versioned corpus would find both versions and have to choose between them.

The human tax lawyer, by contrast, picks up the telephone. Neither the lawyer nor the model can conclude without the date, but the lawyer asks for it, recognizing that a single missing day dictates four terms of the opinion: project eligibility, the reinvestment quota, the compliance deadline, and the minimum holding period, twelve months before the reform and five years after.

\section{Experimental design}

We set out to measure errors that a reader of the answer cannot see.

\paragraph{The task.}

Each item is a tax file presented to the model as a client would submit it to counsel, accompanied by the statutory text in force at the date of the facts. The system prompt is minimal and unguided across all runs:

\begin{quote}
\emph{You are a tax lawyer. A client is consulting you. Answer as you would in a real file.}
\end{quote}

The items, the statutory texts and the system prompt are in French; the prompt is translated here. A complete item, with its statutory text, is in the repository.

The prompt deliberately contains no instruction to identify or request missing information. In legal practice, counsel receiving a new file is not forewarned that a critical fact is absent; identifying factual gaps is an intrinsic outcome of the legal analysis itself. Prompting the model to ask for missing information would implicitly signal the presence of a factual void, thereby bypassing the core capability under test: whether the system independently recognizes when a statutory change renders an unstated fact decisive.

We supply the applicable statutory text directly because \emph{Temporal Misgrounding in Legal RAG}~\cite{cymbler2026} established that models otherwise fail during the retrieval step. Because our evaluation does not target retrieval, we isolate the factual elicitation phase by assuming retrieval is fully solved.

\subsection{Models and runs}

The benchmark evaluates thirteen leading language models, queried between 05:15 and 14:07 UTC on 19 August 2026. Claude Opus 5 was reached through Anthropic's first-party API; GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.6 Terra, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Grok 4.6, Mistral Large 3, Mistral Medium 3.5, and Qwen 3.6 Plus through OpenRouter to their first-party backends; and Kimi K3, GLM-5.2, DeepSeek V4 Pro, MiniMax M3, and Llama 4 Maverick across third-party providers. The last eight have published weights, though all runs used cloud endpoints. Complete identifiers, routing metadata, and timestamps are in the released dataset. Temperature was zero wherever the endpoint supports it; Claude Opus 5 and the two GPT-5.6 variants do not expose the parameter. Three repetitions per item yield 500 responses across 507 cells, after seven API non-terminations. Of the four models that produce the five requests on real-world items, two were routed to a single backend and two across several: the figure is not an artefact of routing.

\subsection{Coding}
\label{sec:coding}

Each response is placed in one of four categories: it requests the missing fact and states no conclusion; it answers, then requests the fact as a condition; it assumes a value and concludes on it; or it does not ask and concludes with no mention that anything is missing. A fifth code, \emph{absent}, marks seven cells where a model produced no response, excluded from every denominator. On a control item nothing is missing, so only two codes can apply, the model answers or requests something it does not need; this held across all 269 coded control cells.

Automatic annotation was performed by Claude Opus 5. \emph{Temporal Misgrounding in Legal RAG}~\cite{cymbler2026} rejected LLM-as-a-judge because a judge scoring legal accuracy would inherit the temporal biases it was meant to detect. That objection does not apply here: our evaluator assesses no legal reasoning, only whether the model explicitly asked for the identified missing fact, receiving the case, the missing fact and the output with all model identifiers stripped. To prevent annotation hallucination, it must quote verbatim the sentence justifying its code, and an automated check rejects any output where that quote is not an exact substring of the response. Although Claude Opus 5 is itself one of the thirteen evaluated models, double-blind human verification found 100\% agreement ($n=13$) on the responses it produced, against 98\% ($n=122$) elsewhere, ruling out self-evaluation bias.

\subsection{Human verification}
\label{sec:verification}

A second author independently coded 135 cells in a double-blind setup, unaware of the automated annotations, model identities, and item labels. Sampling was conducted across three draws: 50 uniform cells to mirror dataset distribution; 40 stratified cells (10 per category) to test category separability; and a targeted draw of 45 cells focused on practitioner-sourced items.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{How often they ask}

Across the five measurement items built from reported court cases, models request the missing fact in only 3\% of responses (5 of 192). In the remaining 97\%, they deliver a legal conclusion.

Only the first behaviour is correct: legal counsel must ask before advising. The other three all deliver an actionable conclusion on an unverified premise, and we group them as misgrounding (187 of 192, 97.4\%).

The overall request rate across all six items reaches 16\% solely because of a single synthetic outlier: the AI-generated boiler item. Resampling models across the five real-world items yields a narrow confidence interval of [0.5\%, 5.1\%], whereas resampling items across the full set yields [0.4\%, 44.4\%]. This extreme width is a structural finding rather than a limitation: the variance resides entirely in the items.

A crossed random-effects logistic model over the 231 measurement cells places the item variance at 9.13 [3.34, 24.94] against 0.27 [0.12, 0.58] for the model. Fitted without the synthetic boiler item, the item variance drops to 1.22 [0.38, 3.94] against 0.45 [0.21, 0.96]. Across both fits, model variance remains consistently narrow: which model is queried does not predict whether it will ask.

\subsection{Model identity does not predict the behaviour}

Four models request the missing fact at all: one twice, three once. The other nine never do. Each model produced fifteen responses on those five items, except MiniMax M3, which produced twelve.

The spread between the best and the worst model does not change the conclusion.

\paragraph{Repetition on the strongest item.}

Each item was put to each model three times. Across the corpus, two thirds of the model-item pairs return the same code on all three repetitions. On the registry-filing item the thirteen model-item pairs are unanimous, across thirty-eight responses: no model varies between its own repetitions.

Blair-Stanek \& Van Durme~\cite{blairstanek2025} report instability of up to 50\% on hard legal questions at temperature zero. We do not claim these models are stable in general, and a third of our pairs are not. On the item that carries the strongest result, the behaviour does not fluctuate.

\paragraph{Manual verification of the strongest figure.}

We read all thirty-eight conclusions on the registry-filing item rather than relying on the automatic coding for the figure that carries the paper. All thirty-eight decide the merits, several under reservation: \emph{subject to the transferred branch qualifying as complete}, \emph{subject to the three-year holding undertaking}. None of these reservations bears on the date that governs which regime applies.

\subsection{Controls}

Seven control items, on which nothing is missing, and the model should answer. It does so in 86.6\% of cases, 233 of 269.

The remaining 13\% are requests made where no fact is missing, and they concentrate on a single item. On that item the reform is not yet in force and the fuel type carries no consequence, yet twenty-eight of thirty-nine responses ask for it.

They ask the question the later version of the text would call for, on facts the earlier version governs. Excluding the boiler, the controls return eight false positives across 230 cells, or 96.5\% correct.

\subsection{The failure survives explicit injunction}\label{sec:ablation}

A reader might hypothesize that models fail to elicit facts simply because our baseline system prompt does not explicitly instruct them to do so. We empirically tested this hypothesis through an ablation study across two representative items, thirteen models, and three repetitions (78 calls, 5 API non-terminations). We appended a single explicit instruction to the system prompt:

\begin{quote}
\emph{If a necessary fact is missing, ask for it before concluding.}
\end{quote}

The two items diverge. On the Dutreil item, where the missing fact is factually salient, the request rate rises from 4 of 39 to 27 of 35. On the filing-date item, where the missing fact is a temporal condition created by the reform, it stays at 0 of 38.

Thirteen leading models, explicitly commanded to ask for missing facts before concluding, produced 38 definitive legal opinions on a filing date none of them possessed.

Two conclusions follow. First, prompt engineering cannot resolve narcissus misgrounding when the factual void is induced by statutory change: if an explicit command yields zero elicitation on filing dates, a gentler instruction is unlikely to succeed where an explicit one does not. Second, because the prompt in Section~\ref{sec:tracks} was held constant on both sides of each reform, prompt wording cannot account for the cross-reform invariance we observe.

\section{Why they do not ask}\label{sec:why}

Each missing fact admits a default value the model can adopt silently. For \emph{dates}, it defaults to the latest statutory text and applies it; for \emph{occupant services} (Dutreil), it assumes a standard furnished lease without hotel services; for \emph{statutory declarations}, it recites the list verbatim without registering that it lacks client values to fill it in; and for the \emph{use of funds}, it answers under reservation, delivering a conclusion while treating the missing fact as a mere hypothetical.

When a default value is available, the model adopts it instead of asking.

This is what makes the error invisible: the response never states that a value was chosen.

\paragraph{The synthetic outlier.}

The boiler item stands apart because its missing fact admits no default, and it alone was AI-generated rather than drawn from a reported case. This may indicate that models pick up structural cues from their training distribution when faced with synthetic inputs, and default to silent assumptions on real-world files.

\paragraph{Observations on the instruments.} Three deployment findings emerged. \emph{Token ceilings}: MiniMax M3 exceeded its 32,000 token limit to return 37,016 reasoning tokens, and generated 79,371 at a 64,000 limit. \emph{Non-termination}: the same model failed to conclude across seven cells, generating reasoning until hitting provider timeouts. \emph{Routing variability}: four failed calls succeeded on identical retries where only the upstream API provider changed, revealing non-deterministic routing in aggregators.

\section{Conclusion}

\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}
Narcissus misgrounding sits strictly downstream of the failure identified in Temporal Misgrounding in Legal RAG~\cite{cymbler2026}. Where that work showed standard retrieval fetching the wrong statutory version, our setup provides the exact, date-conditioned text within the prompt: the model holds the right law, at the right date, and nothing in it is hallucinated or misread. The failure occurs because the missing piece is not a legal norm but a client-specific fact, residing in no corpus. This makes the risk more insidious than retrieval failure or statutory hallucination: an opinion that cites the right law and reasons flawlessly appears pristine, yet rests on a silent factual assumption the reader cannot locate. The greater danger is a well-reasoned, authoritative answer given on facts that are not the client's.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

Five boundaries. \emph{Domain scope}: six French tax regimes, though the mechanism, where a norm change creates an unstated factual requirement, is domain-agnostic. \emph{Prompting regime}: an unguided baseline, with explicit elicitation tested by ablation on two items only. \emph{Multi-fact interaction}: two items miss several facts at once; on the disposal item a date and a sector together induced four-code disagreement ($\kappa = 0.704$), leaving binary agreement unaffected ($\kappa = 1.00$). \emph{Provenance}: one synthetic item against five drawn from reported cases; separating provenance from default availability needs real-world items with no default value. \emph{Single-turn evaluation}: a single API call offers no channel for dialogue, and a model that answers is not necessarily blind to the gap. Multi-turn settings~\cite{dlawbench} would separate the two.
